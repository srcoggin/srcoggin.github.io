NFL teams racing into the free agency window are being warned that the market’s familiar temptations — the shiny wide receiver, the pricey running back and the quick-fix veteran quarterback — can be the fastest way to burn cap space and derail a roster plan.

CBS Sports, in a free agency “playbook” built around 10 rules for front offices, framed this year’s shopping season as one that rewards discipline over splash. The central message is that even teams that believe they have free agency “figured out” can get trapped by predictable mistakes, especially at positions where demand spikes and prices climb quickly.

The quarterback market sits at the center of the conversation. CBS Sports highlighted the value of pursuing bargain options at the position, a nod to the way competent veteran starters can sometimes be acquired without committing premium dollars and multi-year guarantees. For contenders, that approach can preserve flexibility to retain core players and address multiple needs; for rebuilding teams, it can keep the books clean while a longer-term solution is developed through the draft or future offseasons. In a league defined by quarterback play, the difference between a cost-effective bridge and an overpaid patch can reverberate for years.

The playbook also addressed the running back dilemma, a position that has seen teams increasingly resist long-term spending even as elite production remains coveted. CBS Sports pointed to “paying RBs” as one of the defining questions of roster construction, particularly in an era when teams have found contributors on rookie deals and short-term contracts. The implication for fans is straightforward: the same big contract that can stabilize an offense on Sundays can also limit the ability to invest in premium positions elsewhere.

At wide receiver, CBS Sports cautioned against what it described as “the WR wall,” a reference to the point in free agency when prices and expectations can outstrip sustainable roster planning. With receiver salaries rising and bidding wars common, teams can be pressured into deals that look reasonable in March but become inefficient once production, injuries or scheme fit are accounted for.

What makes the guidance notable is its timing. Free agency often reshapes contenders overnight and can rescue struggling rosters, but it also creates dead money and cap headaches when teams chase name value rather than fit and price.
